This file accompanies the manuscript. It contains the Supplementary Methods (S1 to S14), the Supplementary Results (S1 to S4), {s.n_tables} Supplementary Tables and {s.n_figures} Supplementary Figures. Tables and figures are numbered in the order the manuscript first cites them, and the methods and results sections in the order of the analysis. Every table is built from the analysis output files named in its note by a script archived with the code, and every result in this text was read from those files or recomputed from the per-subject results with the test the pipeline uses.

Conventions. Groups are compared by two-sided Mann-Whitney *U* tests with a continuity correction. The effect size is the rank-biserial correlation *r* = 1 − 2*U*/(*n*₁*n*₂) with *U* computed for the reference group, so a positive *r* means higher values in the group compared with it: in patients when patients are compared with controls, and in the HRV-ACC group when either HRV-ACC group is compared with the MIT-BIH records. A note states the convention wherever a contrast differs from this. Partial correlations are regress-then-rank partial Spearman correlations: the measure and the group indicator are each regressed on the covariates by least squares with an intercept, and the residuals are rank-correlated. *p* values are uncorrected unless labeled as adjusted, in which case they are Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted within the family named ({S:families}). In the tables, a blank first cell means the row continues the measure named above it, and any other blank cell means the quantity does not apply to that row.

# Supplementary Methods

## Supplementary Methods S1. Recording protocol, analysis window and onset check

The HRV-ACC recordings were made with the Polar H10 chest strap, which stores R-R intervals and three-axis acceleration (Książek et al., 2023). According to the dataset description, every participant first sat at rest for 5 minutes and was then encouraged to walk. Patients were asked to walk in the ward corridor at the start of the recording and again 30 and 60 minutes later, for about 3, 5 and 7 minutes. No walk was supervised, and seven controls did not receive the walking instruction. The description does not state where the controls were recorded. The release does not mark when walking occurred, so walking was detected from the accelerometer ({SM:movement}). All 60 recordings in the release were analyzed: controls {ids.ctl} and patients {ids.pat}.

Each R-R file was read as exported, and beat times were rebuilt as the cumulative sum of the intervals from the first beat; the wall-clock time stamps in the file were not used to build the time axis. They were used to align the accelerometer ({SM:movement}), to give the time of day of each window and to find transmission gaps ({SM:preprocessing}). The analysis window is the half-open interval from 600 s to 2400 s of beat time, selected before artifact filtering. Its duration is therefore fixed and its beat count is not: over the 60 HRV-ACC recordings the number of clean intervals and mean heart rate correlate at *r* = {hrid.r_all}.

The 600 s skip was chosen after patients' mean heart rates at shorter skips had been inspected (main text Section 2.2), so the analysis was repeated with the skip set to 60 s and 360 s to test whether that choice favored the results (window_skip_sensitivity.py; {S:skip}). The skip barely changed mean heart rate. Patients averaged {s.skip_hr_p_60}, {s.skip_hr_p_360} and {s.skip_hr_p_600} bpm and controls {s.skip_hr_c_60}, {s.skip_hr_c_360} and {s.skip_hr_c_600} bpm at skips of 60, 360 and 600 s. The spectral slope and the VLF/LF ratio were stable across skips; sample entropy, approximate entropy and determinism weakened as the skip lengthened; and network transitivity reached *p* = {s.tra360_p} at 360 s only.

The MIT-BIH records were analyzed over the same span, 600 s to 2400 s after the start of each record. A skip was added for them after an earlier check, run without one, found heart rate raised in the first ten minutes of most records. The same check now compares mean heart rate over the first 600 s of the analyzed window (minutes 10 to 20 of the record) with the remaining 1200 s: the first-minus-remaining difference averaged {s.onset_mean} bpm (SD {s.onset_sd}), was positive in {s.onset_npos} of the 18 records and was not systematic (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, {s.onset_pe}). The record headers give start times from {s.mit_clock_lo} to {s.mit_clock_hi} and no dates, so every analyzed MIT-BIH window fell between about {s.mit_win_lo} and {s.mit_win_hi}. The HRV-ACC files carry the phone's time of day with every interval but no dates. Mapped through those stamps, the HRV-ACC windows began between {tod.c_lo} and {tod.c_hi} in controls and between {tod.p_lo} and {tod.p_hi} in patients, at similar times in the two groups (*r* = {tod.r}, {tod.pe}; {S:gaps}).

## Supplementary Methods S2. Artifact filtering, quality control and the resolution lattice

Each window was cleaned in two passes (reject_ectopic_beats in pipeline.py). Intervals shorter than 300 ms or longer than 2000 ms were removed first. Each remaining interval was then compared with the median of a centered window of 11 intervals, five on each side and truncated at the ends of the series, and removed if it differed from that median by more than 20%. Flagged intervals were deleted rather than interpolated, and beat times were rebuilt as the cumulative sum of the surviving intervals, so the series closes over each deletion. Over all records, the bounds pass removed {s.flag_bounds_c} intervals in controls, {s.flag_bounds_p} in patients and {s.flag_bounds_m} in MIT-BIH records, and the median pass removed {s.flag_median_c}, {s.flag_median_p} and {s.flag_median_m}. No record had more than {s.maxrun_all} consecutive flagged intervals.

Mean artifact rates did not differ between the HRV-ACC groups ({qc.art_c}% in controls and {qc.art_p}% in patients; *r* = {s.art_r}, {s.art_pe}). Because the 20% threshold is a fraction of heart period, it is a larger multiple of beat-to-beat variability in patients. Measured against the RMSSD of the cleaned series, the threshold was a median of {s.thrc_p} times RMSSD in patients and {s.thrc_c} times in controls (*r* = {s.thrc_r}, {s.thrc_pe}); against the RMSSD of the uncleaned window, which the rejected intervals inflate, the medians were {s.thr_p} and {s.thr_c} (*r* = {s.thr_r}, {s.thr_pe}). The filter was therefore about twice as permissive for patients relative to their variability. A deviation just below the threshold would be larger relative to a patient's beat-to-beat variability than to a control's, and the leniency may contribute to the patients' numerically lower artifact rate. A record was to be excluded for an artifact rate above 5% or fewer than 240 clean intervals; none met either criterion. The cleaned series held a median of {s.nrr_med_c} intervals in controls, {s.nrr_med_p} in patients and {s.nrr_med_m} in MIT-BIH records, with a minimum of {qc.n_min}.

For the MIT-BIH records, R peaks were detected in the first signal channel of each record by NeuroKit2's ecg_process at the database's 128 Hz sampling rate (Makowski et al., 2021), and R-R intervals were the differences between successive peak times within the window. The beat annotations distributed with the database were not used. The same two-pass filter followed.

The recording grid was measured rather than assumed (observed_rr_lattice in pipeline.py). The smallest nonzero absolute difference between successive intervals estimates the grid step, and the fraction of differences that are whole multiples of that step tests whether the grid is uniform. Every HRV-ACC series gave a step of 1.000 ms and every MIT-BIH series a step of 7.8125 ms, with every successive difference on the grid in every record. The 1/1024 s unit of the Bluetooth heart rate profile therefore does not describe the exported intervals, which are whole milliseconds, and every resolution control uses the measured 1.000 ms step.

The same time stamps show where intervals are missing (recording_times_and_gaps.py). When the Bluetooth link between the strap and the phone drops, no intervals are recorded and the phone clock runs ahead of the time rebuilt from the intervals. A gap was defined as a clock step exceeding the interval recorded after it by more than {s.gap_thr} s. Elsewhere the excess never exceeded {s.gap_jitter} s and the smallest gap was {s.gap_min} s, so the threshold is not critical. Gaps inside the analysis window were found in {gap.n_rec} recordings ({s.gap_ids}), which lost {gap.min_s} s to {gap.max_min} min of intervals. Because beat time is rebuilt from the intervals, the series is joined across each gap, as it is across deleted artifacts. Without these recordings, the unadjusted differences in sample entropy and SD1/SD2 lost significance and no other primary or heart-rate-adjusted conclusion changed. Dropping as many recordings at random changed a median of {s.gap_rand_med} conclusion, and {s.gap_rand_share}% of random draws changed at least as many ({S:gaps}).

## Supplementary Methods S3. Spectral estimation, band powers and slope estimators

Each cleaned series was interpolated at 4 Hz by cubic spline between its first and last beat times, with each interval placed at the time of the beat that begins it. The spectrum was estimated by Welch's method (Welch, 1967) with 512-sample (128 s) segments, a Hann window, 50% overlap and removal of each segment's mean, which places bins 0.0078 Hz apart; an 1800 s window yields {s.welch_min} or {s.welch_max} segments. Band powers are trapezoidal integrals over the bins that lie inside each band, using the band limits of the Task Force (1996). The narrow gaps between bands (0.039 to 0.047 Hz and 0.148 to 0.156 Hz) therefore contribute to total power but to no band, and VLF, LF and HF power together make up {s.bandsum_lo}% to {s.bandsum_hi}% of total power. The nominal VLF lower limit of 0.003 Hz lies below the lowest resolved bin, so VLF power is integrated from 0.0078 Hz over five bins.

The spectral slope is the negated least-squares slope of log₁₀ power on log₁₀ frequency over the bins inside the fitted range. Over the nominal 0.01 to 0.15 Hz range the realized bins run from 0.0156 to 0.148 Hz (18 bins, 0.98 decades), and the uncorrected slope uses all of them. The baroreflex-excised slope removes the bins from 0.07 to 0.13 Hz and fits the remaining 10. Linear bins are denser at high frequencies on a logarithmic axis, which gives the upper part of the range, where the baroreflex resonance lies, more weight in the uncorrected fit. The log-binned slope averages log power within 9 logarithmically spaced bins (half the number of linear bins, limited to 5 to 12) so that each part of the range carries similar weight. The median *R*² of the uncorrected fit was {s.r2_med_c} in controls and {s.r2_med_p} in patients, and the group difference was larger among fits with *R*² of at least 0.70 ({s.r2_n_c} controls and {s.r2_n_p} patients; {S:estimators}).

The aperiodic-plus-peak model fits log₁₀ power = *c* − β·log₁₀ *f* + *A*·exp(−½((log₁₀ *f* − μ)/σ)²) to the 18 bins by bounded nonlinear least squares, a one-peak form of spectral parameterization (Donoghue et al., 2020). The bounds are β from −2 to 5, *A* from 0 to 5, a peak center 10^μ from 0.06 to 0.14 Hz and a width σ from 0.03 to 0.25 log₁₀ units. A Gaussian as wide as the whole range stops modeling a peak and absorbs the curvature of the spectrum, which is why the width is limited. Fits whose center or width ended within 0.001 of a bound were flagged (8 controls and 13 patients), and the slope is also reported for unflagged fits alone.

IRASA (Wen and Liu, 2016) resampled the interpolated series by factors *h* from 1.05 to 1.50 in steps of 0.05 and by their reciprocals, computed Welch spectra with 1024-sample segments, took the geometric mean of each up- and down-sampled pair and the median across *h* as the fractal component, and fitted the log-binned slope of that component over the same range. IRASA needs the analysis band to extend beyond the evaluated band by the largest resampling factor, and over 0.016 to 0.148 Hz in 30-minute records that headroom is not available. In a simulation recorded in the pipeline documentation (compute_irasa in pipeline.py), IRASA recovered a slope of about 0.67 from a true slope of 1.0 in the presence of a strong 0.1 Hz peak, so it is reported as a sensitivity estimate only.

Further variants changed one setting at a time: linear rather than constant detrending within each Welch segment, which tests whether slow drift inside segments steepens the fit; segments of 256 and 1024 samples (bins 0.0156 and 0.0039 Hz apart, with 9 and 36 bins in the fitted range); linear rather than cubic interpolation; and a family of fitted ranges, of which the two lowest end at 0.047 and 0.062 Hz, below the baroreflex resonance and below any respiratory contribution ({S:estimators,bands}). Finally, a model-free check measured the baroreflex peak in each subject (mayer_residual_check.py): a line was fitted to the subject's log spectrum over 0.0156 to 0.148 Hz with 0.07 to 0.13 Hz excluded, and the mean and largest log₁₀ excess of the spectrum over that line inside the excluded band were recorded. The mean excess averaged {s.resid_c} log₁₀ units in controls and {s.resid_p} in patients (*r* = {s.Mayer_Resid_Mean_r}, {s.Mayer_Resid_Mean_pe}), so the resonance stands further above the trend in controls and flattens their uncorrected slope more.

## Supplementary Methods S4. Detrended fluctuation analysis and multifractal DFA

Detrended fluctuation analysis used NeuroKit2's fractal_dfa (Makowski et al., 2021). The series minus its mean is integrated, the integrated series is divided into windows that overlap by half, each window is detrended by a least-squares line, and the root-mean-square residual *F*(*n*) is computed for each window size *n*; the exponent is the slope of log *F*(*n*) on log *n* (Peng et al., 1995). α₁ used every window size from 4 to 16 beats and α₂ every size from 16 to 64, so size 16 belongs to both. Full-range α used NeuroKit2's default sizes, one tenth of the series length in number, logarithmically spaced from 10 beats to one tenth of the series and rounded to whole beats with duplicates removed. That gave {dfa.nsc_min} to {dfa.nsc_max} sizes per series, with a largest window of {dfa.win_min} to {dfa.win_max} beats.

The short-range exponent α₁ was varied in three ways ({S:scaleband}). The lower bound was moved to 8 and to 10 beats (main Table 4). α₁ was refitted over 4 to 15 beats, so that it shares no window size with α₂, which gave *r* = {s.dfa.DFA_Alpha1_Disjoint} ({s.dfa.DFA_Alpha1_Disjoint_pe}). Finally, time-matched exponents converted 4 to 16 s and 16 to 64 s into beats with each subject's own mean NN interval (lower bound at least 4 beats, at least four sizes), so that every subject's fit covers the same range of time scales and therefore of frequencies. The time-matched short-range exponent did not differ (*r* = {s.dfa.DFA_TimeMatched_4s_16s}, {s.dfa.DFA_TimeMatched_4s_16s_pe}) and the long-range one did (*r* = {s.dfa.DFA_TimeMatched_16s_64s}, {s.dfa.DFA_TimeMatched_16s_64s_pe}), the same pattern as α₁ and α₂.

Multifractal DFA followed Kantelhardt et al. (2002). The integrated series was divided into non-overlapping segments from its start at 20 logarithmically spaced scales from max(8, *N*^¼) to *N*/4 beats ({s.mf_smax_lo} to {s.mf_smax_hi} beats at the largest scale), each segment was detrended by a least-squares line, and the *q*-th order fluctuation function *F*_q(*s*) was the *q*-th root of the mean of the segment variances raised to *q*/2. The generalized Hurst exponent *H*(*q*) is the slope of log *F*_q(*s*) on log *s*. The primary summaries use positive moments only, *q* = 1 to 5: the width Δh = max *H*(*q*) − min *H*(*q*), the mean of *H*(*q*), and *H*(2). Negative moments weight the smallest fluctuations and are unreliable at these series lengths; over *q* = −5 to 5 the width had a coefficient of variation of {s.mf_cv_full} across subjects against {s.mf_cv_pos} for positive moments, so the full-range values are shown only to document that instability ({S:mfdfa}).

The width was also compared with 20 shuffled and 20 phase-randomized surrogates of each series (seeded). Shuffling keeps the amplitude distribution and destroys correlations; phase randomization keeps the power spectrum and therefore the linear correlations, but makes the amplitude distribution Gaussian (Kantelhardt et al., 2002). Each comparison gives a Z-score, (observed width − surrogate mean)/surrogate SD. Widths exceeded their shuffled surrogates in most subjects of both groups (Z above 1.96 in {s.mfz_Shuffle_np} patients and {s.mfz_Shuffle_nc} controls; median Z {s.mfz_Shuffle_p} against {s.mfz_Shuffle_c}; *r* = {s.mfz_Shuffle_r}, {s.mfz_Shuffle_pe}), so in both groups much of the width depends on the order of the intervals rather than on their distribution alone. Patients' widths exceeded their phase-randomized surrogates more often than controls' did (median Z {s.mfz_PhaseRand_p} against {s.mfz_PhaseRand_c}; Z above 1.96 in {s.mfz_PhaseRand_np} patients and {s.mfz_PhaseRand_nc} controls; *r* = {s.mfz_PhaseRand_r}, {s.mfz_PhaseRand_pe}), and the difference held with mean heart rate held constant (partial *r* = {s.mfz_pr_hr_r}, {s.mfz_pr_hr_pe}). This contrast cannot separate nonlinear correlation from a non-Gaussian amplitude distribution, because phase randomization removes both. It was not repeated under the resolution controls, although the grid is coarser relative to beat-to-beat variability in patients and shapes the distribution of their intervals, and it rests on 20 surrogates per subject. It is reported as an unexplained difference rather than as evidence for or against nonlinear dynamics.

## Supplementary Methods S5. Entropy measures

Sample entropy was computed in pipeline.py with an explicit tolerance. For embedding dimension *m* = 2, every pair of templates of length 2 drawn from the first *N* − 2 points is compared by the Chebyshev (maximum-coordinate) distance; *B* counts pairs within the tolerance *r*, *A* counts the pairs that also match at the third point, self-matches are excluded, and sample entropy is −ln(*A*/*B*) (Richman and Moorman, 2000). The tolerance is 0.2 times the standard deviation of the series being analyzed. Approximate entropy is Φᵐ − Φᵐ⁺¹, where Φᵐ averages the log of each template's match fraction with self-matches included (Pincus, 1991), with the same *m* and *r*. Both were recomputed by NeuroKit2 at the same tolerance as a check, and the two implementations agreed to within {s.nk_maxdiff} in every subject.

Permutation entropy is NeuroKit2's entropy_permutation with order 3 and delay 1: the Shannon entropy of the frequencies of the six possible orderings of three successive intervals, divided by log₂(3!) so that it lies between 0 and 1 (Bandt and Pompe, 2002). Tied values are ordered by position, so ties created by the recording grid produce ordinal patterns that a continuous series would not. The ordinal tie rate, the fraction of three-interval windows containing at least one exact tie, measures how often that happens. Orders 4 and 5 were computed as sensitivity checks ({S:tolerance}).

Multiscale entropy is refined composite multiscale entropy (Costa et al., 2005; Wu et al., 2014). At scale τ, the series is coarse-grained by averaging non-overlapping blocks of τ intervals, starting from each of the τ possible offsets; the template counts *A* and *B* are summed over offsets before the logarithm is taken. The tolerance is fixed at 0.2 times the SD of the original series at every scale, so a change across scales reflects the dynamics rather than a shrinking tolerance. A scale is retained only if the coarse-grained series holds at least 100 points, up to τ = 20, and the summary is the mean over retained scales. The mean over the fixed scales 1 to 10 and the slope of entropy on scale are reported in {S:mse}.

## Supplementary Methods S6. Recurrence quantification, recurrence networks and embedding selection

Recurrence quantification embedded each series with dimension 2 and delay 1, so that each point is a pair of successive intervals, and measured Euclidean distances between points (Marwan et al., 2007). Only pairs of points at least *W* samples apart in time are eligible, where *W* is the Theiler window (Theiler, 1986). *W* was set per subject to the delay at the first local minimum of the mutual information between the series and its lagged copy (16-bin histogram, lags 1 to 30), or to the lag of the global minimum when there was no local one, and never below the embedding span of 2. It ranged from {s.tw_c} samples in controls and {s.tw_p} in patients ({S:embedding}), and excluded a median of {s.theiler_excl_med}% of pairs (at most {s.theiler_excl_max}%). The recurrence threshold was set by exact count: the 10% of eligible pairs with the smallest distances are recurrent, so the recurrence rate among eligible pairs is 0.1000 in every subject. Over all pairs, including those inside the Theiler window, the rate ranged from {s.rec_all_lo} to {s.rec_all_hi}. A threshold set as a fraction of the attractor diameter fixes a distance, not a rate, and a distance quantile admits every tied distance at the cut; because the recording grid makes ties common and they are more common in patients, either rule would let the recurrence rate differ between groups. The recurrence matrix is symmetric with an empty main diagonal. Determinism is the fraction of recurrent points that lie on diagonal lines of length 2 or more, and laminarity the fraction on vertical lines of length 2 or more (Marwan et al., 2002). Minimum diagonal lengths of 3 and 4 were checked ({S:embedding}).

The recurrence network reads the same matrix as an adjacency matrix (Marwan et al., 2009; Donner et al., 2010). The local clustering coefficient of a point is the fraction of pairs of its neighbors that are themselves connected (zero for points with fewer than two neighbors), and network clustering is its mean over points. Transitivity is three times the number of triangles divided by the number of connected triples. The mean degree divided by *n* − 1 equals the overall recurrence rate and is fixed by construction, as is the recurrence rate itself, so neither was tested ({S:rna}). The network measures were also recomputed after dithering, after matched coarsening and on the first {qc.n_min} clean intervals of every series, the length of the shortest series, each time with the subject's Theiler window from the primary run (rna_controls.py).

At each subject's own embedding, the delay was the mutual-information delay above, and the dimension was the smallest at which fewer than 1% of nearest neighbors were false (Fraser and Swinney, 1986; Kennel et al., 1992). A neighbor was false when adding the next dimension increased the distance by more than 15 times the original distance, or when the added coordinate differed by more than 2 SD of the series, the attractor-size criterion. The search used the first 1,500 points and dimensions 1 to 10. At these embeddings the Theiler window equals the embedding span, (dimension − 1) × delay + 1 (median {s.tw_sel_c} samples in controls and {s.tw_sel_p} in patients), because the span always exceeds the delay.

## Supplementary Methods S7. Quantization controls and the quantization-noise estimate

At a fixed grid step *q*, the grid is a larger fraction of beat-to-beat variability when variability is small. The step-to-RMSSD ratio expresses that fraction, and the ordinal tie rate ({SM:entropy}) measures one of its consequences. Three controls address it ({S:quant}). Dithering added to every interval a value drawn uniformly between −0.5 and +0.5 ms, which breaks every exact tie without changing the resolution; the group comparison uses one seeded draw, and 20 further draws gave a mean that changed no rank-biserial *r* by more than {s.dither_rep_maxshift}. Matched coarsening re-quantized each series to a step of 0.088 times its own RMSSD when that step exceeded 1 ms, which brings the higher-variability series down to the patients' mean effective resolution (the 1.000 ms step divided by the patients' mean RMSSD of {s.rmssd_mean_p} ms) and leaves the rest unchanged; it coarsened {quant.mq_c} controls and {quant.mq_p} patients. Because it only ever coarsens, it can remove a quantization advantage but cannot create one. Targets of 0.10, 0.15 and 0.20 were applied in the same way and, separately, to the subsample whose observed ratio each target equalizes. Re-quantization rounded every HRV-ACC interval to the nearest multiple of 7.8125 ms and recomputed every measure except full-range DFA α; for the spectral measures the time base was rebuilt from the rounded intervals, and the entropy tolerances were recomputed from the rounded series. A 128 Hz recorder rounds beat times rather than intervals, which makes successive interval errors negatively correlated rather than independent. The pipeline documentation records a comparison of the two rounding models on an earlier version of the data, with amplitude and spectral effect sizes within 0.01 of each other; it was not repeated on the current outputs.

The noise that quantization adds to the slope fit was estimated as white noise of variance *q*²/12. Spread evenly from 0 Hz to the 2 Hz Nyquist frequency of the resampled series, its power within the fitted range is *q*²/12 × (0.15 − 0.0156)/2. Spread only up to half the mean beat rate, the more conservative assumption because the beats are the samples, the same noise is concentrated in a narrower band and contributes more to the fitted range. Relative to each group's mean LF power, the noise at 1.000 ms was {nf.pol_c}% in controls and {nf.pol_p}% in patients under the first assumption and at most {nf.pol_p_beat}% under the second. On the 7.8125 ms grid it was {nf.mit_c}% and {nf.mit_p}%, and at most {nf.mit_p_beat}% (Quantization_Noise_Floor.csv; the beat-rate figures were computed from the same quantities). The nominal 1/1024 s Bluetooth step gives the same figures to two significant digits.

## Supplementary Methods S8. Surrogate testing

For each subject, 100 iteratively amplitude-adjusted Fourier transform surrogates were generated from the cleaned series (Schreiber and Schmitz, 1996), each with 1,000 iterations from a random-phase start, with the random-number generator seeded identically for every subject. Each iteration imposes the Fourier amplitudes of the original series and then restores its exact amplitude distribution by rank-order substitution, so every surrogate has exactly the observed distribution and approximately its power spectrum, while any nonlinear structure is destroyed (Theiler et al., 1992). Sample entropy, DFA α₁ and multiscale entropy were computed on each surrogate with the same settings as on the observed series; because the surrogates share the observed distribution, the entropy tolerance is the same. All 100 surrogates gave valid values in every subject. An observed value outside the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles of its own surrogates is a departure, and the Z-score is (observed − surrogate mean)/surrogate SD. The raw values and the Z-scores of the three measures form a six-test family ({S:surrogate}). A departure means structure beyond the amplitude distribution and linear correlation, which may reflect nonlinear dynamics or nonstationarity; the stationarity diagnostics ({S:stationarity}) bear on the second.

## Supplementary Methods S9. Accelerometer processing, walking bouts and movement-free segments

The accelerometer was processed in accelerometer_mobility.py ({S:accel}). The analysis window, defined in beat time, was mapped to wall-clock time through the R-R file's own timestamps, and the accelerometer samples within those clock times were selected; the two streams share the device clock, but the exported files contain no common event marker, so their alignment could not be verified independently. Gravity and posture were removed from each axis by subtracting a Gaussian-smoothed copy of it (Gaussian SD 10 s), and motion was the Euclidean norm of the three filtered axes. Mobility, the activity covariate, is the mean motion over the window in milli-g, the mobility measure of the dataset authors (Książek et al., 2024). Every subject met the minimum of 500 samples and 50% coverage of the window, and the covariate models were repeated without the two subjects below 80% coverage ({S:covariates}).

Walking bouts were detected on the motion trace smoothed by a Gaussian with SD 15 s. A bout is a run of at least 20 s in which smoothed motion exceeds 2.5 times the subject's own median motion. The threshold is relative so that it marks a person moving more than they usually do, rather than marking habitually restless subjects as walking throughout. Each bout was padded by 30 s before and 180 s after its threshold crossings, because heart rate rises quickly with movement and recovers over minutes. Each beat took the walking label of the nearest accelerometer sample in time.

The movement-free reanalysis (stationary_reanalysis.py) used the longest contiguous run of non-walking beats in each window, because splicing non-adjacent segments would create discontinuities that the spectrum reads as broadband power and DFA as a step. In the primary version, the first 600 raw beats of each run were filtered as in {SM:preprocessing} and every measure was recomputed; runs shorter than 600 beats were excluded. Matching beats gives a patient with a higher heart rate a shorter segment, so a duration-matched version took the first 370 s of each run instead. That version also required at least 500 beats within the 370 s, which admits only subjects whose heart rate in the run was at least about {s.dur_bpm} bpm; it is therefore selected on heart rate as well as underpowered, and is shown for completeness ({S:movementfree}). In both versions the time base was rebuilt from the segment's own intervals, and the short segments average far fewer Welch segments than the full window, so their spectral estimates, VLF power above all, are less precise.

## Supplementary Methods S10. Stationarity diagnostics

Three diagnostics were computed per series ({S:stationarity}). The reverse-arrangement test divides the series into 40 consecutive segments, computes the variance of each and counts the pairs in which an earlier segment has the larger variance; under stationarity that count has a known mean and SD, from which a two-sided *p* value follows (Bendat and Piersol, 2010). The variance ratio is the variance of the second half divided by that of the first. The augmented Dickey-Fuller test, whose null hypothesis is a unit root, used a lag order chosen by AIC (Dickey and Fuller, 1979); the KPSS test, whose null hypothesis is stationarity around a constant, used an automatically chosen lag (Kwiatkowski et al., 1992). Both come from statsmodels, which reports the KPSS *p* value from a table bounded at 0.01 and 0.10, so a value of 0.01 means *p* ≤ 0.01. The two tests have opposite null hypotheses, so agreement between them is more informative than either alone. KPSS rejected stationarity at its table limit in {s.kpss_c} of 30 control and {s.kpss_p} of 30 patient series, and none of the diagnostics differed between groups.

## Supplementary Methods S11. Respiratory peak detection

A spectral peak at the breathing rate cannot be found as the largest value in the HF band, because on a spectrum that falls with frequency the largest value is usually the lowest bin. The detector (respiratory_peak_frequency in pipeline.py) therefore removes the trend first. A line was fitted to log₁₀ power on log₁₀ frequency from 0.0156 to 0.398 Hz with 0.07 to 0.13 Hz excluded, and peaks were sought in the residual from that line. A candidate is a local maximum of the residual between 0.15 and 0.40 Hz that exceeds a threshold derived from the sampling distribution of the Welch estimate. A Welch estimate averaging *K* segments is approximately gamma-distributed around the true spectrum, so its log₁₀ has SD √ψ′(*K*)/ln 10, where ψ′ is the trigamma function. Overlapping segments are correlated, so the effective *K* is smaller than the number of segments; the detector takes it as 0.82 times the number of segments (median {s.keff_med} here). For this supplement, white noise of the same length was analyzed with the same Welch settings, which gave an effective *K* of {s.keff_sim} times the number of segments, so the detector's SD, and with it the threshold, is about {s.keff_inflate}% larger than the nominal α requires and the detector is conservative. The threshold is that SD times the normal quantile for a two-sided α of 0.05 divided by the number of bins searched, so the false-positive rate applies to the whole band; it ranged from {s.resp_thr_lo} to {s.resp_thr_hi} log₁₀ units. The highest candidate gives the respiratory frequency. Local maxima above the same threshold between 0.10 and 0.15 Hz were recorded separately and never labeled respiratory, because slow breathing and the baroreflex resonance cannot be told apart there at this resolution. The detected frequency was used descriptively only ({SR:respiration}).

## Supplementary Methods S12. Statistical procedures

Groups were compared by two-sided Mann-Whitney *U* tests (SciPy mannwhitneyu, method 'auto', continuity correction, tie correction), with the rank-biserial effect size defined in the conventions above ({S:environment}). Bootstrap intervals for *r* resampled subjects with replacement within each group 2,000 times and took the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles; they are not adjusted for multiplicity. The heart-rate-adjusted partial correlations and their change with adjustment were bootstrapped in the same way, with one set of resamples shared by every measure ({S:hr}). Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) was applied within each family of {S:families}, which also lists the families' members and the effective number of independent tests in the primary family (Li and Ji, 2005). Sensitivity analyses report uncorrected *p* values and are read against the primary result rather than as independent findings.

Partial correlations used the regress-then-rank method: the measure and the group indicator were each regressed on the covariates by ordinary least squares with an intercept, and the residuals were correlated by Spearman's method, whose *p* value is reported. The unadjusted comparison is the same statistic without covariates, so an adjusted value is always compared with it rather than with a rank-biserial *r*. In the heart-rate analysis five model forms were compared ({S:hr}): heart rate entered linearly; heart rate and its square; the log of the measure against heart rate; the log of the measure against the log of the mean NN interval; and a fully rank-based form, in which the measure, the group indicator and heart rate were each replaced by ranks before the regression, with the residuals correlated by Pearson's method and the *p* value taken from the *t* distribution with *n* − 3 degrees of freedom. The two methods differ when a covariate relationship is monotone but not linear. The beat-count adjustment used the number of clean intervals in place of heart rate. Normalization divided RMSSD and SDNN by the mean NN interval and each band power by its square (Sacha, 2014).

Parametric checks ({S:primaryx}) used Welch's *t* test, Cohen's *d* with the pooled SD and an approximate 95% interval, and Hedges' *g*. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test within each group and equality of variance by Levene's test centered on the median (Brown and Forsythe, 1974). Equivalence of the two control cohorts was tested by two one-sided *t* tests against a margin of ±0.5 pooled SD (Schuirmann, 1987). The three groups were compared by Kruskal-Wallis tests (Kruskal and Wallis, 1952) with Dunn's tie-corrected post hoc tests (Dunn, 1964), adjusted over all pairwise tests. Counts were compared by Fisher's exact test and differences between correlations by Fisher's *r*-to-*z* transformation. The smallest detectable effect used the normal approximation *d* = (*z*₀.₉₇₅ + *z*₀.₈₀)√(1/*n*₁ + 1/*n*₂), converted to a rank-biserial *r* as 2Φ(*d*/√2) − 1; the noncentral *t* distribution gives *d* = {s.mde_nct_d} (*r* = {s.mde_nct_r}) for 30 against 30 ({S:power}).

## Supplementary Methods S13. Classification procedure

The pre-specified comparison asked whether the uncorrected slope adds to mean heart rate and RMSSD. Both models were logistic regressions with an L2 penalty and C = 1, fixed beforehand, with the features standardized on the training data of each fold, and both were evaluated by leave-one-out AUC. The uncertainty of the AUC difference came from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of subjects in which both models were refitted and scored on the subjects left out of the resample ({S:classifier}).

The nested classifier is a methods demonstration and carries no claim in the main text. Its feature pool was 22 measures, the 21 primary measures and approximate entropy; the construction-fixed recurrence rate was excluded by name, and age, sex, diagnosis and medication, which exist only for patients, were excluded because they would identify the group perfectly. Inside each training fold, features were kept if a Mann-Whitney test on the training subjects gave *p* < 0.05 (normal approximation), with at least three kept. Its performance was estimated by leave-one-out cross-validation; by 2,000 bootstrap resamples of subjects in which the entire pipeline, including selection, was rerun and scored out of bag; and by 100 repetitions of stratified 5-fold cross-validation, whose range is the spread of the pooled AUC across repetitions rather than a confidence interval. A permutation test reran the entire pipeline on 1,000 label shuffles, with *p* = (count + 1)/(1,000 + 1), so it cannot resolve below 0.001. Leave-one-out AUC is biased downward, because removing one subject shifts the class balance of the training set against that subject, which is why the permutation null centers below 0.5. Missing-value screening, removal of constant columns and complete-case filtering were applied to the pooled data before cross-validation; they use no label information. A reduced model removed sample, approximate and multiscale entropy and determinism, the measures the control analyses tied to resolution and heart rate, and used all 18 remaining measures without selection; that exclusion was chosen from this dataset, and its interval came from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of subjects with the model refitted and scored out of bag. A fixed five-measure model (RMSSD, the uncorrected slope, DFA α₂, sample entropy and determinism) involved no selection. The figure of the nested classifier is {SF:classifier}.

## Supplementary Methods S14. Software, random seeds and estimator validation

The pipeline ran in Python {env.python} with NumPy {env.numpy} (Harris et al., 2020), SciPy {env.scipy} (Virtanen et al., 2020), pandas {env.pandas} (McKinney, 2010), scikit-learn {env.sklearn} (Pedregosa et al., 2011), statsmodels {env.statsmodels} and NeuroKit2 {env.neurokit2} (Makowski et al., 2021). Each cohort run writes these versions and its seeds to Environment.csv ({S:environment}). The analysis is seeded rather than deterministic: seed 1234 governs dithering, the MFDFA surrogates and the synthetic series of the estimator validation; seed 42 the IAAFT surrogates and the classifier's cross-validation, bootstraps and permutations; and seed 0 the bootstrap intervals for *r* and for the heart-rate-adjusted correlations, and the random exclusions of {S:gaps}. With these library versions the seeded analysis is repeatable: when the full pipeline was rerun for this version of the manuscript, every per-subject value not affected by a deliberate change of setting matched the previous run exactly.

The DFA and multifractal estimators were validated on exact fractional Gaussian noise synthesized by circulant embedding (Davies and Harte, 1987), for which the DFA exponent and *H*(2) both equal the Hurst exponent *H*. Five series were generated for each combination of *H* = 0.6, 0.7, 0.8 and 0.9 and length 2,000 or 2,500, each with its own seed derived from seed 1234 ({S:validation}). Full-range α, α₂ and *H*(2) recovered *H* to within {val.other_absmax}. α₁ read high by {val.a1_min} to {val.a1_max} at every *H*. The exponents observed here lie above the validated range, so the validation supports the estimators' behavior but not their accuracy at the observed values. The code is available at https://github.com/corbin-smith/ecg-criticality-psychiatry-pilot and archived on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.21794970, all versions). # Supplementary Results

## Supplementary Results S1. Respiratory frequency

A respiratory peak cleared the detection threshold in {s.resp_found_c} of 30 controls and {s.resp_found_p} of 30 patients (Fisher exact {s.resp_found_pe}). Where one was found, its frequency was higher in patients (medians {s.resp_f_c} and {s.resp_f_p} Hz, about {s.resp_bpm_c} and {s.resp_bpm_p} breaths per minute; *r* = {s.resp_f_r}, {s.resp_f_pe}). A peak between 0.10 and 0.15 Hz, below the HF band, was found in {s.resp_low_c} controls and {s.resp_low_p} patients ({s.resp_low_pe}), and in {s.resp_amb_c} controls and {s.resp_amb_p} patients it fell inside the baroreflex band, where it cannot be told apart from the resonance; {s.resp_none_c} controls and {s.resp_none_p} patients showed neither.

These results are descriptive. Detection depends on how far a peak rises above the fitted trend, and the trend fits the two groups' spectra differently, so a difference in detection rate need not be a difference in breathing. The frequencies come from a selected subset, and slower breathing in some controls would place respiratory power inside the slope's fitted range, where it would flatten the control fit. The two lowest fitted ranges (to 0.047 and 0.062 Hz) lie below any respiratory contribution, and the slope difference holds in both ({S:bands}). Respiratory rate was not measured directly and could not be matched between groups, so HF power and DFA α₁, whose window sizes correspond to respiratory frequencies at these heart rates ({S:scaleband}), should be read with that in mind.

## Supplementary Results S2. Covariance structure of the measures

Within each group, the 17 measures that differed in the primary family fall into a few tightly correlated clusters ({S:intercorr}; {SF:intercorr}). Three pairs are identities or near-identities by definition: RMSSD and SD1, SDNN and SD2, and mean NN interval and mean heart rate correlate at ±1.00 in both groups. The vagal cluster of RMSSD, pNN50, HF power and total power was tightly intercorrelated (|*r*| = {s.vag_c_lo} to {s.vag_c_hi} in controls and {s.vag_p_lo} to {s.vag_p_hi} in patients). Approximate and sample entropy correlated at {cor.apen_sampen_c} in controls and {cor.apen_sampen_p} in patients, effectively one measure on two scales. The uncorrected slope, DFA α₂ and full-range α formed a third cluster, more tightly coupled in patients (|*r*| = {s.sc_p_lo} to {s.sc_p_hi}) than in controls ({s.sc_c_lo} to {s.sc_c_hi}). Of the {s.hp_n} pairs with |*r*| above 0.7 in either group, {s.hp_both} exceeded it in both, {s.hp_psych} in patients only and {s.hp_healthy} in controls only.

The slope's relation to the amplitude measures differed between groups. In controls it was nearly uncorrelated with RMSSD ({s.cov_rmssd_c}) and weakly related to total power ({s.cov_tp_c}) and HF power ({s.cov_hf_c}); in patients it correlated negatively with all three ({s.cov_rmssd_p}, {s.cov_tp_p} and {s.cov_hf_p}) and with LF power ({s.cov_lf_p}, against {s.cov_lf_c} in controls). Fisher *r*-to-*z* tests reached *p* = {s.cov_hf_pz} for HF power and *p* = {s.cov_rmssd_pz} for RMSSD, and *p* = {s.cov_tp_pz} and {s.cov_lf_pz} for total and LF power. None would survive correction for the number of pairs examined, so this pattern is hypothesis-generating. It is consistent with the covariate analysis, in which HF power and RMSSD were the covariates that weakened the slope difference most ({S:covariates}).

## Supplementary Results S3. Activity during the recording window

Activity did not differ between groups ({S:activity}). Mobility, the share of the window spent walking, the number and length of walking bouts, the time of the first bout and the motion outside bouts were all similar, and {s.nobout_c} controls and {s.nobout_p} patients had no detected bout. The walking instruction falls inside the window for patients, about 30 minutes into the recording, but it was neither supervised nor given to seven controls, so these measurements, rather than the protocol, describe the activity that the analyses control.

The longest movement-free run held a median of {s.run_c_med} beats in controls and {s.run_p_med} in patients (range {s.run_lo} to {s.run_hi}; *r* = {s.run_r}, {s.run_pe}). Higher heart rate accounts for part of that difference, since a run of the same duration holds more beats in a faster heart. The rest reflects runs that covered a larger share of the window in patients (medians {s.runf_p_med}% and {s.runf_c_med}% of the window's beats), a difference that was not significant on its own (*r* = {s.runf_r}, {s.runf_pe}). Runs of at least 600 beats were available for {s.run600_c} controls and {s.run600_p} patients, so the primary movement-free reanalysis excluded {s.mf_excl_c} controls and {s.mf_excl_p} patients ({S:movementfree}). In those segments heart rate was still higher in patients (*r* = {s.mf_hr_r}, {s.mf_hr_pe}), so the movement-free analysis controls walking but not the heart-rate difference. In the duration-matched version the excised slope still differed (*r* = {s.mfd_betax_r}, {s.mfd_betax_pe}) and the VLF/LF ratio did not ({s.mfd_vlflf_r}, {s.mfd_vlflf_pe}), the same pattern as at 600 beats.

## Supplementary Results S4. Embedding selection

The selected embeddings differed between subjects more than between groups ({S:embedding}). The median selected dimension was {emb.dim_c} in controls and {emb.dim_p} in patients (*r* = {emb.dim_r}, {emb.dim_pe}), and the dimension search reached its limit of 10 in {s.emb_m10_c} controls and {s.emb_m10_p} patients. The mutual-information delay had no local minimum within 30 lags in {s.emb_d30_c} controls and {s.emb_d30_p} patients, and its first local minimum fell at a lag of 3 or less in {s.emb_d3_p} patients and no controls (Fisher exact *p* = {s.emb_d3_fe}). Because the same delay sets the Theiler window of the primary analysis, those {s.emb_d3_p} patients were analyzed with a window of 2 or 3 samples, against at least 7 in every control. Their step-to-RMSSD ratios did not differ from those of the other patients ({s.short_ratio_pe}), and without them the fixed-embedding determinism difference was larger, not smaller (*r* = {s.det_wo_short_r}, {s.det_wo_short_pe}; 30 controls and {s.det_wo_short_n} patients; computed for this supplement), so the short windows do not explain its direction.

At each subject's own embedding, determinism and laminarity were higher in both groups than at dimension 2 and did not differ between groups. At the fixed embedding, longer minimum line lengths enlarged the determinism difference ({rqa.lmin3} at 3 and {rqa.lmin4} at 4). A determinism difference that depends on the embedding and on the line length in this way describes the recurrence construction as much as the series, which is why the main text draws no conclusion from it.
