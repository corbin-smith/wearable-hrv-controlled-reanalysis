# Abstract

**Background:** Reduced heart rate variability (HRV) in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder is well documented, and wearable HRV is increasingly proposed for psychiatric monitoring and classification. Heart rate is raised by illness and treatment, yet nonlinear HRV measures are rarely tested against heart rate or recording resolution. **Methods:** The open HRV-ACC dataset was reanalyzed: {n_pat} medicated inpatients ({n_sz} with schizophrenia, {n_bp} with bipolar disorder) and {n_ctl} controls of similar age and sex recorded with the same chest strap, with {n_mit} MIT-BIH recordings as an independent reference. Each of 21 primary measures was tested against mean heart rate, re-quantization to the 7.8 ms grid of a clinical ECG, estimator settings, movement, age and sex. **Results:** Every amplitude measure was lower in patients and kept most of that difference with heart rate held constant (RMSSD partial *r* {abs.rmssd_u2} unadjusted and {abs.rmssd_a2} adjusted). Spectral power shifted toward the lowest frequencies: the very-low-frequency share of total power rose from {vlf.share_c}% to {vlf.share_p}%, and the low-frequency spectral slope steepened (*r* = {abs.slope_r2}). The slope survived every control, and the very-low-frequency shift survived all but direct control of movement. Sample entropy and the recurrence measures did not survive adjustment for heart rate, because over a fixed window the beat count is heart rate. Re-quantization removed the sample and approximate entropy differences, multiscale entropy did not survive surrogate normalization, and three estimator choices changed a conclusion. Mean heart rate and RMSSD alone separated the groups with a leave-one-out AUC of {abs.auc_base}. **Conclusions:** In medicated inpatients, the wearable HRV differences that survive these controls are differences in amplitude and in the distribution of spectral power, not a separable difference in complexity. Heart-rate adjustment and recording resolution should be reported with any such measure.

# 1 Introduction

Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder are accompanied by reduced heart rate variability (HRV). Meta-analyses report large reductions in vagally mediated HRV in schizophrenia, with an RMSSD Hedges' *g* of −0.91 across 24 comparisons (Clamor et al., 2016), and in bipolar disorder, with an overall *g* of −1.77 across 10 comparisons (Faurholt-Jepsen et al., 2017). The direction is consistent, but both meta-analyses report very high heterogeneity, and an umbrella review that re-graded 21 systematic reviews covering 442 primary studies rated the evidence for reduced HRV as suggestive in schizophrenia and weaker in bipolar disorder (Wang et al., 2025). The question matters clinically: people with severe mental illness die earlier than the general population, and cardiovascular disease is a major contributor (Correll et al., 2017).

Wearable chest straps make HRV cheap to record on a psychiatric ward, and the recordings reanalyzed here have already been used in three ways by the team that collected them. HRV correlated negatively with the general psychopathology subscale of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) in these patients (Książek et al., 2024); a convolutional nearest-neighbor classifier separated patients from controls with 85% accuracy and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.92 (Buza et al., 2023); and a deep-learning classifier reported 83% accuracy under five-fold and 80% under leave-one-out validation (Książek et al., 2025).

Other measures describe the structure of the heart rhythm rather than its amplitude. Earlier studies reported reduced nonlinear complexity of the heart rhythm in acute schizophrenia (Bär et al., 2007) and in bipolar mania (Henry et al., 2010), without reporting an adjustment for mean heart rate [[FLAG: confirmed for Henry et al. (2010); confirm for Bär et al. (2007)]]. The low-frequency spectral slope, which summarizes how variability is distributed across time scales, is steeper after myocardial infarction and in the denervated hearts of transplant recipients (Bigger et al., 1996). Measures of this kind are being proposed as markers of illness, so what they measure matters.

Three routes can produce a group difference in an HRV measure that is not a difference in autonomic regulation. The first is heart rate. HRV depends on heart rate both physiologically and mathematically (Monfredi et al., 2014; Sacha, 2014; de Geus et al., 2019), and antipsychotic treatment can raise heart rate (Leung et al., 2012). Over a recording of fixed duration, a faster heart also produces more beats, so any measure that depends on series length depends on heart rate as well. The second is resolution. A chest strap transmits each interval on a fixed grid, and the grid is a larger fraction of beat-to-beat variability in people whose variability is small. Entropy and recurrence measures count near-identical patterns and are sensitive to that fraction. The third is estimator choice. Embedding dimension, fitting ranges and frequency bands are set by the analyst, and a result that holds at one setting and reverses at a neighboring one describes the setting rather than the patients. Recording patients and controls on different hardware would add a device difference to all three; in the data used here both groups wore the same device.

This study tests each route directly. It uses the HRV-ACC recordings (Książek et al., 2023, 2024), in which patients and controls were recorded by the same team with the same chest strap under a common protocol, and the MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm Database (Goldberger et al., 2000) as an independent reference recorded on different hardware. Twenty-one primary measures, covering amplitude, spectral distribution, fractal scaling, entropy and recurrence, were compared between groups and then tested again with mean heart rate held constant, after re-quantization to the resolution of a clinical recording, and across estimator settings. Every patient was medicated and hospitalized, so the comparison describes treated inpatients rather than either illness on its own, and it makes no diagnostic or biomarker claim. The aim was to establish which group differences in wearable HRV remain once heart rate, recording resolution and estimator choice are controlled.

# 2 Materials and Methods

## 2.1 Data and participants

The HRV-ACC dataset (Książek et al., 2023) holds R-R intervals and accelerometer data recorded with the Polar H10 chest strap from psychiatric inpatients and healthy controls. Version 2 of the record adds subject-level age, sex, ICD-10 diagnosis and quetiapine exposure, which are used here; its R-R and accelerometer files are identical to those of version 1. The original study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Silesia (BNW/NWN/0052/KB1/135/I/22/23), and all participants gave written informed consent.

The patient group comprised {n_pat} inpatients of the Psychiatric Department in Tarnowskie Góry: {n_sz} with schizophrenia (F20) and {n_bp} with bipolar affective disorder (F31), aged {age.p_min} to {age.p_max} years, {age.p_female} of them women. The control group comprised {n_ctl} healthy volunteers recorded by the same team on the same device, aged {age.c_min} to {age.c_max} years, {age.c_female} of them women. The groups were of similar age ({age.c_mean} ± {age.c_sd} against {age.p_mean} ± {age.p_sd} years; *r* = {age.r}, {age.pe}) and sex composition (Fisher exact {age.sex_pe}), in line with the dataset authors' description of a similar age and sex structure; controls were not individually matched (Table 1). All 60 HRV-ACC recordings in the release were analyzed; their identifiers are listed in {SM:protocol}.

The two diagnoses were analyzed as one patient group because both are associated with reduced HRV (Clamor et al., 2016; Faurholt-Jepsen et al., 2017) and because dimensional models place psychotic disorders and bipolar I disorder on a shared spectrum (Kotov et al., 2017). Whether pooling changed any result was tested directly (Section 2.4).

The metadata record quetiapine exposure ({cohort.quet} of {n_pat} patients) and no other agent, so patients without quetiapine were receiving other medication, not none. Further clinical detail, including other agents and doses, was requested from the dataset authors and could not be released. A comorbid substance-use code (F19, F19.2 or F13.2) was carried by {cohort.comorb} patients, all with schizophrenia. Quetiapine exposure is concentrated in the bipolar subgroup ({cohort.quet_bp} of {n_bp}, against {cohort.quet_sz} of {n_sz}), and all {n_bp} bipolar patients are women. PANSS scores are included in the release and were not analyzed here. This study addresses between-group differences; associations between HRV and PANSS in these patients have been reported by the dataset authors (Książek et al., 2024).

The MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm Database (doi:10.13026/C2NK5R), distributed through PhysioNet (Goldberger et al., 2000; Pollard et al., 2026), contributed {n_mit} long-term ambulatory ECG recordings from adults without significant arrhythmia, aged {age.m_min} to {age.m_max} years, {age.m_female} of them women. They were sampled at 128 Hz, so their R-R intervals lie on a 7.8125 ms grid. The cohort differs from HRV-ACC in device, resolution, recording setting, era and age at once. It therefore serves only as an independent reference: the patient comparison was repeated against it, and the two control cohorts were compared to see how much each measure moves when everything except health status changes.

## 2.2 Recording window and preprocessing

For every recording, the first 600 s were discarded and the following 1800 s were analyzed (minutes 10 to 40). In the HRV-ACC protocol all participants first sat at rest for 5 minutes and were then encouraged to walk: patients were asked to walk in the ward corridor at the start and again 30 and 60 minutes later, for about 3, 5 and 7 minutes, no walk was supervised, and seven controls were not given the walking instruction (Książek et al., 2023). The first walk therefore falls before the window, the second inside it and the third after it. The window is a mixed-activity recording rather than a resting one, and movement was measured and controlled directly (Section 2.4; {SM:protocol}). The 600 s skip was not fixed in advance. It was chosen after patients' mean heart rates at skips of 60 and 360 s had been inspected for the after-effects of the first instructed walk. Because a patient-group quantity informed the choice, the spectral, heart-rate, entropy and recurrence contrasts were recomputed at all three skips ({S:skip}). Patients' mean heart rate changed little across them ({skip.hr_p_60}, {skip.hr_p_360} and {skip.hr_p_600} bpm), and no contrast was larger at 600 s than at both shorter skips by more than {skip.max_gain} in *r*, so the chosen skip did not favor the differences reported here. The same skip was applied to the MIT-BIH records after a check found heart rate raised in their first ten minutes.

Intervals outside 300 to 2000 ms were removed, followed by any interval deviating by more than 20% from the median of a centered 11-beat window. Flagged intervals were deleted rather than interpolated, so that no synthetic values entered the entropy and recurrence estimates. Mean artifact rates were {qc.art_c}% in controls, {qc.art_p}% in patients and {qc.art_m}% in MIT-BIH records. The highest rate in any record was {qc.art_max}% and the shortest cleaned series held {qc.n_min} intervals, so no record met the exclusion thresholds (an artifact rate above 5% or fewer than 240 clean intervals), and all {qc.n_all} recordings were analyzed ({SM:preprocessing}).

Each HRV-ACC interval also carries a time-of-day stamp. Analysis windows began between {tod.c_lo} and {tod.c_hi} in controls and between {tod.p_lo} and {tod.p_hi} in patients, at similar times in the two groups (*r* = {tod.r}, {tod.pe}). The stamps also show where the Bluetooth link dropped: {gap.n_rec} recordings ({gap.n_c} controls and one patient) lost {gap.min_s} s to {gap.max_min} min of intervals inside the window, and the time axis rebuilt from the intervals joins the series across each gap. Without these recordings, {gap.n_changed} of the {gap.n_tests} primary and heart-rate-adjusted conclusions changed: the unadjusted differences in sample entropy and SD1/SD2 lost significance, as they did in {gap.sampen_share}% and {gap.sd1sd2_share}% of random exclusions of as many recordings ({S:gaps}).

The Polar H10 transmits R-R intervals in units of 1/1024 s through the Bluetooth heart rate service [[FLAG: cite the Bluetooth GATT Specification Supplement (Heart Rate Measurement characteristic), which defines the 1/1024 s unit; the Heart Rate Service v1.0 document does not]], but the intervals exported in this dataset are whole milliseconds. A lattice check found every HRV-ACC series on a 1.000 ms grid and recovered the 7.8125 ms grid of the MIT-BIH records. All resolution controls use the measured 1.000 ms step.

## 2.3 Measures

Twenty-one measures formed the primary family (Table 2). They cover time-domain amplitude (mean NN interval, mean heart rate, SDNN, RMSSD and pNN50, with the Poincaré descriptors SD1, SD2 and SD1/SD2), spectral power and its distribution (LF, HF and total power, the LF/HF ratio and the low-frequency spectral slope), fractal scaling (detrended fluctuation analysis, DFA), entropy (sample, permutation and multiscale entropy) and recurrence (determinism and laminarity). Very-low-frequency (VLF) power, the VLF/LF ratio, approximate entropy, the baroreflex-excised slope and two recurrence-network measures were analyzed alongside them. {S:measures} gives every definition and parameter; the choices that matter for the controls are described here.

Spectra were estimated by Welch's method on R-R series resampled to 4 Hz by cubic spline, with 512-sample (128 s) Hann segments, 50% overlap and constant detrending, which places frequency bins 0.0078 Hz apart. Band powers used the conventional limits (Task Force, 1996). At this resolution the VLF band (nominally 0.003 to 0.04 Hz) integrates the five bins from 0.0078 to 0.039 Hz, LF covers 0.04 to 0.15 Hz and HF 0.15 to 0.40 Hz.

The spectral slope is the negated slope of log power against log frequency, so that a 1/*f* spectrum gives a slope of 1. Bigger et al. (1996) fitted it over 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻² Hz in 24-hour recordings; a 30-minute window cannot resolve that range, so here it was fitted over the 18 Welch bins from 0.0156 to 0.148 Hz, the realized form of a nominal 0.01 to 0.15 Hz range. That range contains the baroreflex (Mayer-wave) resonance near 0.1 Hz, whose prominence could differ between groups and would flatten the fit. The fit was therefore repeated with 0.07 to 0.13 Hz excised, leaving 10 of the 18 bins. This baroreflex-excised slope is reported first in the Results, and the uncorrected fit, which is the member of the primary family, is reported beside it. The slope summarizes how power is distributed across the VLF and LF bands and is not read as an exponent of the whole spectrum (Section 3.5).

DFA used first-order detrending in half-overlapping windows (Peng et al., 1995). The full-range exponent α was fitted over the NeuroKit2 default scales, {dfa.nsc_min} to {dfa.nsc_max} log-spaced window sizes from 10 beats to about one tenth of each series (largest window {dfa.win_min} to {dfa.win_max} beats); α₁ was fitted over 4 to 16 beats and α₂ over 16 to 64 beats. Sample entropy (Richman and Moorman, 2000) and approximate entropy (Pincus, 1991) used embedding dimension 2 and tolerance 0.2 × SD. Multiscale entropy was computed as refined composite multiscale entropy (Costa et al., 2005; Wu et al., 2014) and summarized as the mean over scales 1 to 20 whose coarse-grained series held at least 100 points, which kept all 20 scales in every recording except one control, which kept {mse.ret_c_min}, and one MIT-BIH record, which kept {mse.ret_m_min} ({S:mse}); permutation entropy (Bandt and Pompe, 2002) used order 3 and delay 1 ({SM:entropy}).

Recurrence quantification (Marwan et al., 2002, 2007) used delay embedding with dimension 2 and delay 1, kept from earlier versions of this analysis; Section 3.4 reports each subject's own embedding. The recurrence threshold was set by exact count, so that 10% of eligible pairs are recurrent for every subject. Quantized intervals produce tied distances, and a threshold set as a fraction of the attractor diameter or as a distance quantile would let the recurrence rate drift with the number of ties, which differs between groups. Pairs closer in time than a Theiler window, set per subject at the first minimum of the mutual information function (median {rqa.theiler_c} samples in controls and {rqa.theiler_p} in patients), were excluded. Determinism and laminarity used a minimum line length of 2. Recurrence-network clustering and transitivity were computed from the same matrix (Marwan et al., 2009; Donner et al., 2010). The recurrence rate and the mean network degree are fixed by this construction and were not tested ({SM:recurrence}). Multifractal DFA (Kantelhardt et al., 2002), stationarity tests and respiratory peak detection are described in {SM:fractal,stationarity,respiration}; stationarity diagnostics did not differ between groups ({S:stationarity}).

## 2.4 Control battery

**Heart rate.** Each of 21 measures (the six amplitude measures RMSSD, SDNN and VLF, LF, HF and total power; the VLF/LF and LF/HF ratios; both slopes; the three DFA exponents; four entropy measures; and four recurrence measures) was compared between groups as a partial Spearman correlation with group, holding mean heart rate constant (Table 3). The measure and the group indicator were each regressed on mean heart rate by ordinary least squares with an intercept, and the residuals were rank-correlated. The unadjusted baseline is the same statistic with no covariate, so the two compare directly. Over a fixed 1800 s window the number of beats is close to mean heart rate multiplied by 30, so adjusting for series length and adjusting for heart rate are nearly the same adjustment; both were computed. The model form was varied: heart rate entered as a quadratic, the measure log-transformed against heart rate and against log mean NN interval, and a fully rank-based partial correlation. Amplitude measures were also divided by mean NN interval, or by its square for spectral powers, a normalization proposed because HRV scales with interval length (Sacha, 2014). Heart rate is plausibly a consequence of group membership, through illness or treatment. An adjusted estimate is therefore a decomposition, the part of a difference not carried linearly by heart rate, and not a causal estimate with a confounder removed.

**Resolution.** Three controls address the recording grid. Dithering added uniform noise one 1.000 ms step wide to every interval, which breaks the exact ties created by the grid without changing its resolution; the comparison uses one seeded draw, and the mean of 20 further draws changed no effect size by more than {quant.dither_rep_max}. Matched coarsening re-quantized the higher-variability recordings to a common step-to-RMSSD ratio of 0.088 (the 1.000 ms step divided by the patients' mean RMSSD), which coarsened {quant.mq_c} controls and {quant.mq_p} patients; it is applied only in that direction, so it can remove a quantization advantage but not create one. Targets of 0.10, 0.15 and 0.20 were each evaluated on the subsample they equalize ({S:quant}). Re-quantization placed every HRV-ACC interval on the 7.8125 ms grid of the MIT-BIH records and recomputed every measure except full-range DFA α, to ask whether each difference would be resolvable at the resolution of a 128 Hz ECG. The noise that quantization adds to the spectral fit was estimated as white noise of variance *q*²/12, spread either up to the Nyquist frequency of the resampled series or, more conservatively, up to half the mean beat rate, integrated over the fitted range and expressed relative to mean LF power ({SM:quantization}).

**Estimator choice.** Determinism and laminarity were recomputed at each subject's own embedding, with the delay at the first minimum of mutual information and the dimension chosen by false nearest neighbors (Fraser and Swinney, 1986; Kennel et al., 1992). The lower bound of the DFA α₁ fit was moved from 4 to 8 and to 10 beats. The slope was refitted by five estimators ({SM:spectral}): linear-bin least squares, log-binned least squares, baroreflex-excised, an aperiodic-plus-peak model and IRASA (Wen and Liu, 2016). It was also refitted over a family of frequency ranges, two of which lie below both the baroreflex resonance and any respiratory contribution. Sample entropy was recomputed at tolerances from 0.10 to 0.35 × SD. Sample entropy, multiscale entropy and DFA α₁ were compared on Z-scores against 100 iteratively amplitude-adjusted Fourier transform surrogates (Schreiber and Schmitz, 1996) of each subject's own series ({SM:surrogates}), which asks whether a difference exceeds what the amplitude distribution and linear autocorrelation predict. A choice was counted as changing a conclusion when it moved a result across *p* = 0.05 or produced significant results of opposite sign (Table 4).

**Other confounders.** Physical activity was measured from the accelerometer as mean gravity-removed acceleration, the mobility measure of the dataset authors (Książek et al., 2024), and entered as a covariate. Walking bouts were detected per subject (motion above 2.5 times the subject's median for at least 20 s, padded by 30 s before and 180 s after to cover heart-rate recovery; {SM:movement}; {S:accel}). Every measure was then recomputed on the first 600 beats of each subject's longest contiguous movement-free segment, in subjects whose segment was long enough. A duration-matched version (370 s) retained {mf.dur_n} of 60 subjects and is reported in the supplement as underpowered. Age and sex were entered as covariates. The patient comparison was repeated within each diagnostic subgroup, without the {cohort.comorb} patients with a substance-use code, with quetiapine as a covariate and with exposed patients removed. Finally, it was repeated against the MIT-BIH controls, with age as a covariate because that cohort is younger. Table 5 collects these checks for the two spectral-distribution measures.

## 2.5 Statistics

Groups were compared by two-sided Mann-Whitney *U* tests with a continuity correction ({SM:statistics}). Effect sizes are rank-biserial correlations, *r* = 1 − 2*U*/(*n*₁*n*₂), with *U* computed for the control group, so that positive *r* means higher values in patients. In comparisons with the MIT-BIH cohort, positive *r* means higher values in the HRV-ACC group, and paired values are given as controls against patients unless labeled. Bootstrap 95% intervals, for *r* and for the heart-rate-adjusted partial correlations and their change with adjustment, resampled subjects within each group 2,000 times (percentile intervals) and are not corrected for multiplicity. Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) was applied within families defined by analysis type ({S:families}): the 21 primary measures, the 21 heart-rate-adjusted tests, the slope estimators, the band sweep, the six surrogate-normalized tests, and the multifractal and recurrence-network measures. Approximate entropy, which correlates with sample entropy at *r* = {cor.apen_sampen_c} in controls and {cor.apen_sampen_p} in patients, is reported outside the primary family. Normality and equality of variance were checked, and the primary comparisons were repeated with Welch *t* tests ({S:primaryx}).

Partial correlations are always compared with the unadjusted partial correlation computed the same way, never with a rank-biserial *r*, because the two are different statistics. Their *p* values are those of the Spearman correlation of the residuals. All *p* values are given to three decimals or as *p* < 0.001. With 30 subjects per group, the smallest effect detectable with 80% power at α = 0.05 is *d* = {mde.d} (rank-biserial *r* = {mde.r}) by the normal approximation, with the floors for subgroup contrasts in {S:power}; smaller effects are reported but a null result below this size is not read as evidence of no difference.

One classification comparison was specified in advance: whether the spectral slope adds to the two strongest conventional measures. A logistic regression (L2 penalty, C = 1, fixed beforehand) on mean heart rate and RMSSD was compared with the same model plus the uncorrected slope by leave-one-out AUC, with a bootstrap interval on the AUC difference in which both models were refitted in each of 10,000 replicates. A nested classifier that selects among 22 measures inside each training fold is reported in the supplement as a methods demonstration only ({S:classifier}; {SM:classifier}). Every stochastic step was seeded.

## 2.6 Software and validation

The pipeline was written in Python {env.python} with NumPy {env.numpy} (Harris et al., 2020), SciPy {env.scipy} (Virtanen et al., 2020), pandas {env.pandas} (McKinney, 2010), scikit-learn {env.sklearn} (Pedregosa et al., 2011), statsmodels {env.statsmodels} and NeuroKit2 {env.neurokit2} (Makowski et al., 2021), and it records these versions and its random seeds with every set of results ({S:environment}; {SM:software}). Sample and approximate entropy were cross-checked against NeuroKit2 at matched tolerance. The DFA and multifractal estimators were checked on synthetic fractional Gaussian noise with Hurst exponents from {val.h_min} to {val.h_max} (series of {val.n_list} points, {val.reps} repetitions per setting). Full-range DFA α, α₂ and the multifractal H(2) recovered the true value to within {val.other_absmax}, while α₁ read high by {val.a1_min} to {val.a1_max} ({S:validation}). The exponents observed here lie above the validated range (median α₂ {val.a2_med_c} in controls and {val.a2_med_p} in patients), so the validation supports the estimators' behavior but not their accuracy at these values. Reporting follows the GRAPH guideline for HRV research in psychiatry (Quintana et al., 2016); the completed checklist gives the location of each item ({S:graph}).

# 3 Results

## 3.1 Cohorts and unadjusted differences

Before any control was applied, patients differed from controls on most measures. All {qc.n_all} recordings passed quality control (Table 1). Patients had a higher mean heart rate (median {g.MeanHR.mp} against {g.MeanHR.mc} bpm in controls, {g.MeanHR.pct}; *r* = {g.MeanHR.r}; Figure 1A) and lower variability on every amplitude measure. After correction within the primary family, {g.n_sig} of the 21 measures differed (Table 2), with effect sizes from |*r*| = {g.sig_min} to {g.sig_max} (raw *p* values, Welch *t* tests and distribution diagnostics in {S:primaryx}; effect sizes for every measure in {SF:effects}; distributions in {SF:boxplots}). Mean heart rate exceeded 100 bpm in {clin.hr100_p} patients and {clin.hr100_c} controls, and RMSSD was below 10 ms in {clin.rmssd10_p} patients and {clin.rmssd10_c} control. Between group medians, LF, HF and total power fell by {g.LF.pctabs}%, {g.HF.pctabs}% and {g.TP.pctabs}% (group spectra in Figure 2A), and RMSSD by {g.RMSSD.pctabs}%. The LF/HF ratio did not differ (*r* = {g.LFHF.r}, adjusted {g.LFHF.pae}), so within the LF and HF bands the loss was proportional. The two recurrence-network measures, tested as a separate family, did not differ (clustering *r* = {rna.clu_r}, transitivity *r* = {rna.tra_r}; adjusted {rna.clu_pae} and {rna.tra_pae}; {S:rna}).

pNN50 sat at a floor in patients: {pnn.zero_p} of {n_pat} recorded exactly zero and {pnn.lt1_p} were below 1%, against {pnn.zero_c} controls at zero ({S:clinical}). SD1/SD2 cleared the correction threshold narrowly (adjusted {g.SD1SD2.pae}), and its numerator and denominator are tied by definition to RMSSD and SDNN, so it is not interpreted separately. Several primary measures are near-duplicates: SD1 is RMSSD/√2, SD2 is determined by SDNN and SD1, and mean heart rate and mean NN interval are near-reciprocals. The {g.n_sig} significant results are therefore not {g.n_sig} independent findings: without SD1, SD2 and mean heart rate, {nr.n_sig} of 18 differ ({S:nonredundant}), and the correlations among the measures are given in {S:intercorr} and {SF:intercorr} ({SR:covariance}). The smallest effect this design can detect with 80% power is *r* = {mde.r}; two of the significant measures (sample entropy, *r* = {g.SampEn.r}, and SD1/SD2, *r* = {g.SD1SD2.r}) and all four of the non-significant ones fall below it. The same two lost significance when the four recordings with transmission gaps were left out (Section 2.2).

## 3.2 Heart rate

Holding mean heart rate constant left the amplitude and spectral-distribution differences in place and reduced the sample entropy, approximate entropy and recurrence differences to near zero. Over the fixed 30-minute window, the number of analyzed beats and mean heart rate correlated at *r* = {hrid.r_all} across the 60 HRV-ACC recordings ({hrid.r_c} in controls and {hrid.r_p} in patients; Figure 1A). Patients contributed more beats (mean {hrid.beats_p} against {hrid.beats_c} in controls) because their hearts beat faster, and for every one of the 21 measures, adjusting for beat count and adjusting for mean heart rate gave partial correlations within {hrid.maxdiff} of each other. In this design a control for series length is a control for heart rate.

With mean heart rate held constant, every amplitude measure kept a large and significant association with group (Figure 1B; Table 3). RMSSD moved from a partial correlation of {hr.RMSSD.u} to {hr.RMSSD.a} ({hrb.RMSSD.a_ci}; {hr.RMSSD.pe}; adjusted {hr.RMSSD.pae}). The amplitude measures kept {hr.amp_pct_min}% to {hr.amp_pct_max}% of their unadjusted association, and the bootstrap interval for the change with adjustment excluded zero for all six, so heart rate did carry part of each difference. The result did not depend on how heart rate entered the model. Across the linear, quadratic, rank-based and two log-transformed forms ({S:hr}), RMSSD ranged from {hr.RMSSD.form_lo} to {hr.RMSSD.form_hi}, and every amplitude measure stayed significant (*p* ≤ {hr.amp_form_pmax}) under every form. Dividing by mean NN interval gave the same picture: RMSSD divided by mean NN was lower in patients (rank-biserial *r* = {nn.RMSSD}), as were the normalized spectral powers (*r* = {nn.pow_lo} to {nn.pow_hi}).

The spectral-distribution measures did not weaken. The VLF/LF ratio moved from {hr.VLFLF.u} to {hr.VLFLF.a} and the baroreflex-excised slope from {hr.BETAX.u} to {hr.BETAX.a}, and the uncorrected slope and DFA α₂ also moved slightly away from zero (Table 3); for all four, the bootstrap interval for the change with adjustment included zero. These measures carry information about group that mean heart rate does not.

Sample entropy, approximate entropy and the four recurrence measures did not survive. Sample entropy moved from {hr.SampEn.u} to {hr.SampEn.a} and determinism from {hr.DET.u} to {hr.DET.a}, and approximate entropy, laminarity and the two recurrence-network measures also fell to near zero (Table 3). Of the 21 adjusted tests, {hr.n_sig} remained significant after correction within the family; these six did not (adjusted *p* from {hr.six_padj_min} to {hr.six_padj_max}). Because beat count is heart rate here, these measures cannot be separated from the group difference in heart rate at this sample size. That is not a demonstration that their differences are artifacts. Heart rate is itself one of the group differences, and the adjustment removes whatever part of a measure's difference runs through it.

Equalizing series length by design rather than by adjustment pointed the same way. On the first 2,000 clean intervals of each subject ({fb.n_c} controls and {fb.n_p} patients), sample entropy gave *r* = {fb.SampEn.r} ({fb.SampEn.pe}), approximate entropy {fb.ApEn.r} ({fb.ApEn.pe}), determinism {fb.DET.r} and laminarity {fb.LAM.r}, none significant ({S:fixedbeats}). Equal beat counts mean unequal durations here, because 2,000 beats take longer at a lower heart rate, so this check is reported as a consistency check rather than as a separate estimate.

Multiscale entropy kept a reduced but significant association ({hr.MSE.u} to {hr.MSE.a}, adjusted {hr.MSE.pae}) and kept it at 2,000 beats (*r* = {fb.MSE.r}, {fb.MSE.pe}); the patient curve lies below the control curve at every scale ({SF:mse}). Permutation entropy strengthened ({hr.PermEn.u} to {hr.PermEn.a}), but it reversed on the clinical grid and was not significant in the primary movement-free analysis or against the MIT-BIH controls (Sections 3.3 and 3.5), so it is not treated as a robust difference.

Two associations appeared only after adjustment and survived the family correction: the LF/HF ratio ({hr.LFHF.u} to {hr.LFHF.a}, adjusted {hr.LFHF.pae}) and DFA α₁ ({hr.A1.u} to {hr.A1.a}, adjusted {hr.A1.pae}). Both are reported as exploratory. Adjusting for a variable that group membership itself changes can create associations that the unadjusted data do not contain, and α₁ is unstable for other reasons (Section 3.4).

## 3.3 Recording resolution

The 1.000 ms resolution of the wearable did not explain any difference, but on the coarser grid of a 128 Hz ECG the sample, approximate and permutation entropy results would have changed. At the measured 1.000 ms step, the grid was a larger fraction of beat-to-beat variability in patients (median step-to-RMSSD ratio {res.ratio_p} against {res.ratio_c} in controls; *r* = {res.ratio_r}), and more of their three-beat ordinal patterns contained an exact tie (median {res.tie_p}% against {res.tie_c}% in controls; *r* = {res.tie_r}).

At that resolution the grid did not account for the group differences ({S:quant}). Dithering and matched coarsening changed the rank-biserial *r* of the four recurrence measures by at most {quant.rec_maxshift} and that of multiscale entropy by at most {quant.mse_maxshift}, while sample, approximate and permutation entropy moved away from zero (permutation entropy furthest, from {quant.PermEn.obs} to {quant.PermEn.dith} under dithering). Every shift was away from zero or below 0.01, the opposite of what a quantization artifact would produce. Matched coarsening did not equalize the ordinal tie rate (*r* = {quant.tie_mq_r} after matching), so it removes the grid's advantage only in part.

Re-quantizing every recording to the 7.8125 ms grid of a 128 Hz recording separated the measures (Figure 3; {S:requant}). On that grid, patients' RMSSD spanned a median of {rq.steps_p} grid steps (minimum {rq.steps_p_min}) against {rq.steps_c} in controls, and a median of {rq.tie_p}% of patients' three-beat patterns contained a tie, against {rq.tie_c}% in controls. The sample entropy difference collapsed ({q.SampEn.n} to {q.SampEn.q}, {q.SampEn.pqe}) because the coarse grid removes the near-matches that a low-variability series contains, lowering control values and raising patient values. Approximate entropy behaved the same way ({q.ApEn.n} to {q.ApEn.q}). Permutation entropy reversed sign ({q.PermEn.n} to {q.PermEn.q}, {q.PermEn.pqe}), because a grid that ties most adjacent intervals collapses the diversity of ordinal patterns. The recurrence measures changed without a common pattern: determinism grew ({q.DET.n} to {q.DET.q}), laminarity barely changed, and both network measures lost their direction ({S:requant}). Multiscale entropy was stable ({q.MSE.n} to {q.MSE.q}, {q.MSE.pqe}). DFA α₁, which did not differ at 1.000 ms, differed on the coarse grid ({q.A1.n} to {q.A1.q}, {q.A1.pqe}).

## 3.4 Estimator choices

Of the estimator choices in Table 4, three changed a conclusion: the lower bound of the DFA α₁ fit created one, and the per-subject embedding and surrogate normalization each removed one.

DFA α₁ did not differ over its default range of 4 to 16 beats (*r* = {est.a1_4}, {est.a1_4pe}). Moving the lower bound to 8 beats gave *r* = {est.a1_8} ({est.a1_8pe}), and moving it to 10 beats gave {est.a1_10} ({est.a1_10pe}): a six-beat change produced one of the larger effects in the battery, in the opposite direction to the primary estimate. At these heart rates, 4 to 16 beats corresponds mostly to the respiratory band, whereas 16 to 64 beats lies inside the slope's fitted range ({S:scaleband}). α₁ was also the one quantity on which the two healthy cohorts differed after correction (MIT-BIH {cc.a1_m} against HRV-ACC {cc.a1_h}; *r* = {cc.a1_r}, adjusted {cc.a1_pae}; {S:controls}). No conclusion is drawn from α₁ in either direction.

Determinism differed between groups at the fixed embedding (*r* = {emb.det_fixed}, {emb.det_fixed_pe}; adjusted {g.DET.pae} in the primary family) and not at each subject's own embedding (*r* = {emb.det_sel}, {emb.det_sel_pe}; {S:embedding}; {SR:embedding}). The selected dimensions did not differ significantly between groups, and laminarity behaved the same way ({emb.lam_fixed} at the fixed embedding, {emb.lam_sel} at each subject's own). Longer minimum line lengths enlarged the fixed-embedding difference (*r* = {rqa.lmin3} at a minimum of 3 and {rqa.lmin4} at 4, uncorrected), so the primary choice of 2 was not the most favorable one.

Multiscale entropy differed in raw form (adjusted {sn.mse_raw_pae} within the six-test surrogate family) but not on surrogate-normalized Z-scores (*r* = {sn.mse_z_r}, adjusted {sn.mse_z_pae}; {S:surrogate}; {SF:surrogates}). Where a subject's observed value departed from its own surrogates, it lay below them in both groups, in every such subject for multiscale entropy and in most for sample entropy. Both groups carry structure of the same kind, and the multiscale entropy difference reflects the amplitude distribution and linear autocorrelation that the surrogates preserve rather than a difference in nonlinear complexity.

The remaining choices did not change a conclusion. The slope difference held under every estimator (*r* = {est.slope_min} to {est.slope_max}; {S:estimators}), including the aperiodic-plus-peak model restricted to fits that did not reach a parameter bound ({est.clean_r}, {est.clean_n_c} controls and {est.clean_n_p} patients). ## 3.5 What survives the controls

Two kinds of difference survived the controls: lower amplitude, and a shift of spectral power toward the lowest frequencies, in full for the spectral slope and for the VLF/LF ratio in all but direct control of movement. The reduction in spectral power was not uniform across bands. Between group medians, VLF power fell by {vlf.pct_vlf}% ({vlf.vlf_c} against {vlf.vlf_p} ms²; *r* = {vlf.vlf_r}), against {vlf.pct_lf}% for LF and {vlf.pct_hf}% for HF power. The share of total power in the VLF band therefore rose from a median of {vlf.share_c}% in controls to {vlf.share_p}% in patients, the LF share fell from {vlf.lfshare_c}% to {vlf.lfshare_p}%, and the HF share was similar ({vlf.hfshare_c}% and {vlf.hfshare_p}%). The VLF/LF ratio was higher in patients (median {vlf.ratio_p} against {vlf.ratio_c} in controls; *r* = {vlf.ratio_r}; {S:vlf}). VLF power and the VLF/LF ratio were not pre-specified primary measures; they decompose the difference in total power, which was.

The spectral slope summarizes the same shift. With the baroreflex band excised it was steeper in patients (median {sl.x_mp} against {sl.x_mc} in controls; *r* = {sl.x_r}), and the uncorrected fit gave *r* = {g.BETA.r} (Table 2). The difference held in every range below 0.15 Hz, including the two that lie below both the baroreflex resonance and any respiratory contribution (0.016 to 0.047 Hz, *r* = {band.verylow_r}; 0.016 to 0.062 Hz, mean {band.low_mp} against {band.low_mc} in controls, *r* = {band.low_r}), and it disappeared when the fit was extended over the whole spectrum to 0.40 Hz (*r* = {band.full_r}, {band.full_pe}; Figure 2B; {S:bands}). The slope therefore describes the distribution of power within the VLF and LF bands, not a property of the spectrum as a whole. Neither group's spectra follow a single power law over the fitted range: both depart from the relation β = 2α − 1 expected of one (mean residuals against DFA α₂ of {id.res_c} in controls and {id.res_p} in patients; group difference *r* = {id.res_r}, {id.res_pe}; {S:identity}; {SF:identity}). ## 3.6 Classification against published work

A logistic model on mean heart rate and RMSSD alone separated the groups with a leave-one-out AUC of {cl.auc_base}. Adding the uncorrected slope raised it to {cl.auc_beta}, an increment of {cl.dauc} (95% bootstrap interval {cl.dauc_lo} to {cl.dauc_hi}; {cl.dauc_pe}) that cannot be distinguished from zero. A nested classifier selecting among 22 measures inside every training fold reached a leave-one-out AUC of {cl.auc_nested}; refitted on bootstrap resamples of subjects and scored out of bag, its AUC was {cl.auc_nested_boot} (95% interval {cl.auc_nested_lo} to {cl.auc_nested_hi}; {S:classifier}; {SF:classifier}), an interval that includes the two-feature baseline.

The dataset authors report 85% accuracy and an AUC of 0.92 for a convolutional nearest-neighbor classifier (Buza et al., 2023), and 83% (five-fold) and 80% (leave-one-out) accuracy for a deep-learning classifier (Książek et al., 2025), both working on the interval series of these recordings. Those figures are not directly comparable with the AUCs here: the metrics differ, the validation schemes differ, and their classifiers learn from interval sequences rather than from summary measures. This analysis does not test what drives those classifiers. It shows only that two conventional measures, one of them mean heart rate, already separate these groups well, so any classifier trained on these recordings is best reported against that baseline. Because the comparison is between medicated inpatients and healthy volunteers, none of these results bears on diagnosis.

# 4 Discussion

## 4.1 Summary

In {n_pat} medicated inpatients and {n_ctl} controls recorded on the same chest strap, the HRV differences that survived controls for heart rate, recording resolution and estimator choice were differences in amplitude and in the distribution of spectral power. Every amplitude measure was lower in patients and kept most of that difference with heart rate held constant, under every model form tested. The share of power at the lowest resolvable frequencies was higher in patients; the low-frequency slope that summarizes it survived every control, and the VLF/LF ratio survived all but direct control of movement. The nonlinear measures did not survive as differences in complexity. Sample entropy, approximate entropy and the recurrence measures fell to near zero once heart rate was held constant, because over a fixed window the beat count is heart rate. Re-quantization to a clinical grid removed the sample and approximate entropy differences and reversed permutation entropy, and multiscale entropy, which survived both of those controls, did not survive surrogate normalization. Three estimator choices changed a conclusion: one created a difference and two removed one. Mean heart rate and RMSSD alone separated the groups with a leave-one-out AUC of {abs.auc_base}, and adding the slope did not improve it significantly.

## 4.2 What wearable HRV reflects in medicated inpatients

Mean heart rate was higher in patients by about {g.MeanHR.diff_med} beats per minute at the median. Treatment, illness and the inpatient setting may each contribute, and this dataset cannot apportion them. What it can show is how the other measures relate to heart rate. The amplitude differences exceed what heart rate predicts: they keep {hr.amp_pct_min}% to {hr.amp_pct_max}% of their association after linear adjustment, remain significant under five model forms and after normalization by mean NN interval, so the reduced variability in these patients is not simply a consequence of faster hearts (Monfredi et al., 2014; Sacha, 2014). The reduction is also large by the standard of the literature: RMSSD Hedges' *g* was {g.RMSSD.hg} here ({S:primaryx}), against a pooled −0.91 in schizophrenia (Clamor et al., 2016). The sample entropy, approximate entropy and recurrence differences do not exceed what heart rate predicts. Once heart rate is held constant they cannot be distinguished from zero; multiscale entropy kept part of its difference but did not survive surrogate normalization, and permutation entropy did not survive the clinical grid. That does not make them artifacts, because heart rate itself differs between the groups. It means that at this sample size these measures add nothing that mean heart rate does not already carry. It also does not show that earlier reports of reduced complexity in schizophrenia and bipolar mania (Bär et al., 2007; Henry et al., 2010) reflect heart rate, since their measures, recordings and patients differ from these; it shows that the question has to be asked before a complexity difference is interpreted.

Medication is the leading alternative explanation for every difference reported here. Studies that included medication-free patients found reduced HRV relative to controls (Mujica-Parodi et al., 2005; Bär et al., 2007), although in one of them (Mujica-Parodi et al., 2005) the reduction was larger in patients taking atypical antipsychotics, and a meta-regression found no effect of medication status on vagal indices (Clamor et al., 2016), which argues that the reductions are not purely pharmacological. That evidence does not transfer to a fully medicated sample: it shows that unmedicated patients differ from controls, not that medication contributes nothing here. The quetiapine analysis narrows the explanation for the slope without resolving it, and it does not address the other agents these patients received.

These recordings have also been used to relate HRV to symptom severity, with HRV correlating negatively with the PANSS general psychopathology subscale (Książek et al., 2024). Whether that association persists with heart rate held constant is not known. The released data include the PANSS scores, so the question can be answered.

The practical consequence for wearable studies is that a complexity measure computed on a recording of fixed duration is partly a heart-rate measure. A comparison between groups that differ in heart rate, as medicated psychiatric patients and healthy volunteers do, will move it whether or not the underlying dynamics differ.

## 4.3 Resolution and estimator choices

The resolution result sets a floor. At the sensor's 1.000 ms step, quantization did not explain the differences. At the 7.8 ms step of a 128 Hz ECG, patients' beat-to-beat variability spans about one grid step, most of their three-beat patterns tie, and sample, approximate and permutation entropy stop measuring the dynamics: the first two lose their difference and the third reverses. Multiscale entropy, which coarse-grains the series before matching, and every amplitude and spectral measure were stable. Entropy measures of this kind need an interval resolution well below the beat-to-beat variability of the population studied, and low-variability groups, such as medicated psychiatric patients, are where that condition fails first. Wearable devices export intervals at different resolutions, so the step-to-RMSSD ratio should be reported with any entropy or recurrence measure.

The estimator results generalize in the same way. A short-scale DFA estimate that changes sign when the lower bound of the fit moves by six beats, and a determinism difference present at one embedding and absent at each subject's own, describe the analysis settings rather than the patients. At these heart rates the α₁ range covers mainly respiratory frequencies, and respiratory rate could not be matched between groups ({SR:respiration}). A study that reports a difference in either quantity without a sensitivity analysis may be reporting a setting.

## 4.4 The spectral redistribution

The positive result is a shift of the remaining variability toward the lowest resolvable frequencies: VLF power fell least, and its share of total power rose. The slope is a compact summary of that shift rather than an independent finding, and two observations limit how it can be read. First, it is confined to the VLF and LF bands and disappears over the full spectrum, and neither group's spectra follow a single power law over the fitted range, so the slope is not an exponent of the spectrum and says nothing about the dynamical regime that produced it. The steeper slopes reported after myocardial infarction and cardiac denervation were fitted over 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻² Hz in 24-hour recordings (Bigger et al., 1996), a range this window cannot resolve, so the two are not directly comparable. Second, VLF power is the least reliably estimated of the conventional bands in short recordings (Task Force, 1996); over this 30-minute window it integrates five spectral bins. The VLF/LF ratio also weakened under direct control of movement, and in movement-free segments (median {mf.beats_dur} s) each spectrum averages far fewer Welch segments, so these data cannot decide whether movement or estimation noise weakened it. The excised slope, fitted across the VLF and LF bands together, held under the same controls.

The sources of VLF power are less settled than those of the LF and HF bands. Thermoregulatory and hormonal influences have been proposed (Task Force, 1996), and VLF rhythms also depend on parasympathetic outflow (Taylor et al., 1998), so the smaller loss of VLF power does not by itself point to a non-vagal source. The slope's attenuation under adjustment for HF power shows that the redistribution overlaps with the loss of vagally mediated variability, but the data do not identify which slow influence is preserved. The mechanism is left open.

## 4.5 Recommendations for wearable HRV in psychiatry

The results suggest six reporting practices for wearable HRV in psychiatric samples:

1. Report mean heart rate, and heart-rate-adjusted estimates, alongside every HRV measure.
2. Treat a fixed-duration window as a heart-rate-dependent beat count, and report the beat count.
3. Report the device's interval resolution and the step-to-RMSSD ratio, and avoid sample, approximate and permutation entropy when variability spans only a few grid steps.
4. Report estimator parameters, with at least one sensitivity analysis for embedding and fitting range.
5. Benchmark any classifier against mean heart rate plus RMSSD.
6. Require surrogate normalization before describing a difference as one of nonlinear complexity.

The dependence of HRV on heart rate is established in the methods literature (Monfredi et al., 2014; Sacha, 2014), and how best to correct for it is still debated (de Geus et al., 2019). The value of these recommendations here is that each one changes a conclusion, or for resolution would at the grid of a 128 Hz ECG, in recordings that are already being used for psychiatric classification.

## 4.6 Limitations

Every patient was medicated and hospitalized, and the dataset records only quetiapine. Patients without it were receiving other agents, so no unmedicated comparison exists, and treatment, illness and setting cannot be separated. The slope and VLF/LF results held in the {cohort.nonquet} patients without quetiapine and with quetiapine as a covariate (Table 5), which narrows the medication explanation without resolving it. Smoking was not recorded; it is common in schizophrenia (de Leon and Diaz, 2005) and reduces HRV (Dinas et al., 2013; Murgia et al., 2019). Respiratory rate could not be matched. The walking instruction was unsupervised and was not given to seven controls, so activity varied within as well as between groups; it was measured and controlled, not standardized. With 30 subjects per group the minimum detectable effect is *r* = {mde.r}, so null results are weak evidence, and the bipolar subgroup ({n_bp} patients, all women, with {cohort.quet_bp} of the {cohort.quet} quetiapine exposures) cannot be examined on its own. Each subject was recorded once, so the findings cannot be related to treatment response or course, and the PANSS scores in the release were not analyzed, so they are not related to symptom severity either.

The MIT-BIH cohort differs from HRV-ACC in several ways at once, so it bounds a device effect rather than isolating one. VLF power is coarsely estimated over 30 minutes, and the VLF/LF ratio is sensitive to how movement is handled. The estimator validation covers Hurst exponents of {val.h_min} to {val.h_max}, below the median exponents observed here. The analysis was not preregistered, and three choices were made after the data had been examined: the onset skip, the baroreflex-excised slope as the slope reported first, and the VLF measures added to decompose the slope result. The sensitivity analyses for each ({S:skip,estimators,vlf}) bound their influence but cannot remove it. Finally, adjustment for heart rate is a decomposition, not control of a confounder, because heart rate is plausibly a consequence of group membership.

# 5 Conclusions

In medicated inpatients with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder recorded on a wearable chest strap, HRV is reduced in amplitude and shifted toward the lowest frequencies, and both differences exceed what heart rate predicts. The nonlinear complexity measures do not show a separable difference: sample entropy and the recurrence measures cannot be distinguished from the group difference in heart rate, sample, approximate and permutation entropy fail at the resolution of a clinical recording, and multiscale entropy does not survive surrogate normalization. These findings describe treated, hospitalized patients compared with healthy volunteers, not either illness independent of its treatment, and they support no diagnostic use. For wearable HRV in psychiatry, the answer to the title's question is that heart rate has to be reported and adjusted for before any complexity measure is read. Separating illness from treatment will need a first-episode or unmedicated cohort with repeated recordings.

The HRV-ACC data were collected with the approval of the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Silesia (BNW/NWN/0052/KB1/135/I/22/23), and all participants gave written informed consent to the original study. The MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm Database is distributed openly by PhysioNet. Written informed consent for the present analysis was not required, in accordance with the national legislation and institutional requirements.
